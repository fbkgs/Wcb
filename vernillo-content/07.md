# 17. The REALLY good questions Marlie should ask him

She does not need to ask all of these. Pick **four or five** depending on where the conversation goes.

### Best opening research question

> **"What are the main research questions the lab is working on right now, and where would an undergraduate student fit into those projects?"**

This is probably #1.

It changes the conversation from theoretical interest to **"how can I become useful?"**

---

### Very good follow-up

> **"What would my first few months in the lab typically look like? Would I initially shadow other students, learn specific testing protocols, help with participant recruitment, or start with data processing?"**

Shows she's already imagining herself doing the work.

---

### Another excellent one

> **"What techniques would you expect an undergraduate in the lab to eventually become competent with?"**

Then if he mentions TMS, EMG, dynamometry, participant testing, etc., she can ask follow-ups.

---

### Excellent question connected to the paper

> **"The ultra-marathon paper mentions that female athletes are still underrepresented in the literature. Is the effect of biological sex something you're planning to investigate in the current projects here?"**

Very strong because it proves she read his work. 

---

### Another research-minded question

> **"You discuss both laboratory and real-world field research in ultra-endurance athletes. How do you balance experimental control in the lab with ecological validity when you're trying to study something as complex as ultra-marathon performance?"**

That's genuinely a good scientific question.

He could talk for ten minutes on that one.

---

### A fantastic conceptual question

> **"When you're studying neuromuscular fatigue after prolonged exercise, what measurement do you find most informative for distinguishing central from peripheral mechanisms?"**

She doesn't have to know the answer.

That's literally the point of asking him.

---

### Good undergraduate-development question

> **"What separates undergraduate students who become really valuable members of your research group from students who struggle?"**

PI psychology hack. He tells her exactly what he values.

Then she can actually do those things.

---

### If she's interested in doing an honours thesis

> **"If I joined the lab and things went well, is there a pathway from assisting on existing projects into an honours thesis or independent project?"**

This is particularly reasonable because his current page explicitly advertises undergraduate and honours-thesis supervision. 

---

### Good question about scientific ownership

> **"Do undergraduate students usually have opportunities to become involved in data analysis, conference presentations or manuscripts once they've developed enough experience?"**

Not:

> "Will I get published?"

Subtle difference. Huge difference.

---
# 18. Questions I would NOT have her ask immediately

Don't open with:

> "Is this paid?"

> "How many hours do I have to do?"

> "Will I get my name on a paper?"

> "Will this help me get into med school?"

All perfectly legitimate practical questions **eventually**, but terrible as the first impression.

Similarly, don't spend the meeting trying to prove she's memorized every paper he's ever written.

Professors can smell academic cosplay from orbit.

---
# 19. One especially good conversation she could have about his paper

If he asks what interested her, she could say:

> "I found the idea of ultra-marathon as a physiological model more interesting than I expected. Initially I thought of the research mainly in terms of athletic performance, but the paper reframed it as a way of studying the limits of homeostasis under prolonged stress. I also liked that the implications extend from performance into health, injury and even occupational physiology. The part I'm still trying to understand better is how researchers experimentally separate central fatigue from peripheral fatigue."

This answer does **three things**:

1.  shows she read it; 
2.  shows she can synthesize it; 
3.  deliberately gives him something to teach her. 

That's exactly the type of conversation she wants.
