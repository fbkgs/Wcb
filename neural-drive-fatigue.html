Neural Drive

Biologically, neural drive is the nervous system’s command telling a muscle how strongly to contract.

Think of the pathway like this:

Motor cortex
→
spinal cord
→
α-motor neurons
→
skeletal muscle fibres
→
force

The actual biology starts in the motor cortex. Neurons there increase their firing when you voluntarily decide to contract a muscle. Their axons descend through the corticospinal tract and influence motor neurons in the spinal cord.

Those α-motor neurons are the final common pathway to skeletal muscle. Each motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it controls form a motor unit.

Your nervous system increases muscle force mainly in two ways:

1

Motor-unit recruitment — activating more motor neurons/muscle fibres.

2

Rate coding — making already-active motor neurons fire action potentials more frequently.

So when someone says "increased neural drive to the quadriceps," they basically mean:

The CNS is providing a stronger excitatory command to the quadriceps motor-neuron pool, causing more motor units and/or higher firing rates.

Once the motor neuron fires, the electrical signal travels down its axon to the neuromuscular junction. Acetylcholine is released, triggering an action potential in the muscle fibre. That signal travels along the sarcolemma and T-tubules, causes the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release Ca²⁺, and calcium allows actin–myosin cross-bridge cycling to produce force.

So the whole chain is roughly:

Brain command
↓
Descending corticospinal activity
↓
Spinal motor-neuron activation
↓
Motor-unit recruitment + firing frequency
↓
Neuromuscular junction
↓
Muscle action potential
↓
Ca²⁺ release
↓
Muscle contraction

Where this becomes important for fatigue

You could have a muscle that is still physically capable of producing more force, but the nervous system is not fully activating it.

That's where central fatigue comes in.

During severe or prolonged exercise, inputs from the brain, spinal cord, and sensory feedback from the working muscles can alter the motor command. The nervous system may reduce the amount of activation reaching the muscle, so force falls partly because of reduced neural drive, not solely because the muscle fibres themselves are exhausted.

That's essentially what Vernillo's paper means when it says central fatigue can involve the CNS and sensory signals from muscles/tendons reducing neural drive to skeletal muscle, thereby reducing force-production capacity.

A useful distinction:

Central fatigue:
Brain/spinal cord isn't activating the muscle as strongly.

Peripheral fatigue:
The signal arrives, but processes within the muscle are less capable of producing force.

For her meeting, a really clean definition would be:

"Neural drive is the net motor command from the central nervous system to the motor-neuron pool controlling a muscle. It determines how many motor units are recruited and how rapidly they fire, which ultimately influences muscle force."

That's the proper physiological version.
