# 15. Questions I think Dr. Vernillo is likely to ask her

I'm going to give you both the question and a strong answer direction.

### "Tell me a little bit about yourself."

She shouldn't recite her résumé.

A good answer:

> "I'm studying Integrative Biology at Augustana. I've found that I've enjoyed the research side of my degree most — designing or following protocols, collecting measurements and then figuring out what those measurements actually mean. I've had laboratory experience through Pond Pro and independent field research, and I've presented research several times at the student academic conference. I haven't worked in human-performance research before, which is actually why I'm interested in this opportunity. I'd like to take the research skills I've developed and move into human physiology."

---

### "Why are you interested in my lab?"

This one matters **a lot**.

> "What interests me most is the question of how different physiological systems interact when people are pushed toward fatigue. Your ultra-marathon paper made that particularly clear to me because the performance response isn't purely muscular or cardiovascular — there are neuromuscular, metabolic, thermoregulatory and other systems interacting. I'm especially interested in the neuromuscular side and in learning how techniques such as TMS can help distinguish central from peripheral components of fatigue. I also like that the research has practical implications for training, recovery, injury prevention and potentially other physically demanding populations."

Strong because every sentence connects to his work.

---

### "What did you take away from the ultra-marathon paper?"

I'd have her prepare this one especially well.

> "My main takeaway was that you can use ultra-marathons as a model of extreme physiological stress rather than only studying them as a sporting event. I understood the paper as having three main arguments: they let us study adaptation to extreme loads, they can reveal potentially harmful physiological changes, and the findings can improve training and injury-prevention strategies. I also found the interaction between those three areas interesting — especially the fact that fatigue and recovery involve multiple physiological systems rather than one limiting variable."

Then if the conversation continues:

> "The lack of female-specific research also stood out to me, particularly because female participation is increasing."

That is an **A+ undergraduate response**.

---

### "What is neuromuscular fatigue?"

> "I'd describe it as an exercise-induced reduction in the neuromuscular system's ability to produce force. My understanding is that it can involve central mechanisms, such as reduced neural drive from the nervous system, and peripheral mechanisms occurring in the muscle itself. One thing I'd like to learn in the lab is how those different components are experimentally separated and quantified."

That final sentence is key.

It converts "I don't know everything" into intellectual curiosity.

---

### "Do you have any experience with TMS?"

Do **not** bullshit.

> "No, I don't have hands-on experience with TMS yet. I've been reading about how it's used to stimulate the motor cortex and investigate corticospinal function and fatigue, but learning the method properly is one of the things that attracts me to the lab."

Exactly what a PI wants from an inexperienced undergraduate.

---

### "What laboratory experience do you have?"

Now she can flex a little.

> "My most direct experience was as a laboratory and research summer student with Pond Pro. I carried out water-quality testing including pH and turbidity measurements and worked on comparative research involving chemical agents. The subject matter obviously differs from human physiology, but it taught me to be careful with protocols, measurements and record keeping. I've also done independent environmental field research where I collected quantitative photometric measurements."

And then:

> "So I already understand that reliable research often comes down to being consistent and careful with things that might seem repetitive."

That last line is PI catnip.

---

### "What would you say your strengths are as a researcher?"

> "I'd say reliability, attention to detail and communication. I've worked independently during field courses, but I've also worked in environments where I have to coordinate with other people. Presenting research repeatedly has also made me comfortable explaining results and answering questions. I'm also comfortable admitting when I don't understand something and then going away and learning it rather than pretending I do."

Excellent answer.

---

### "Tell me about a time something didn't work in an experiment."

She should prepare **one real story** from Pond Pro or her field research.

Use:

**problem → what she checked → what she changed → outcome → lesson.**

Something like:

> "During field measurements, consistency between sampling conditions was important, so when a result looked inconsistent with the surrounding measurements, I didn't immediately treat it as meaningful. I checked the measurement conditions and equipment and repeated it where appropriate. It taught me that unexpected data are interesting, but you first need to rule out measurement error."

Only use this if that's truthful to what actually happened. She should substitute her real example.

---

### "What do you want to get out of joining the lab?"

Bad answer:

> "It will look good for grad school."

Good answer:

> "Initially, I'd like to become competent with the basic experimental procedures and understand how human-performance studies are designed and conducted. Longer term, I'd like to become useful enough to contribute more independently — whether that's participant testing, data processing, literature work or eventually developing a research question of my own."

Beautiful progression:

**learn → contribute → independence.**

---

### "Do you know what you want to do after your bachelor's?"

It's fine if she doesn't.

> "I'm still exploring exactly where I want to specialize, and that's part of why research experience like this is important to me. I know that I'm interested in biology and physiology and that I've enjoyed doing research. I'd like enough meaningful experience to determine whether graduate research in human or exercise physiology is something I want to pursue."

Much better than manufacturing a fake ten-year plan.

---

### "Why human physiology if your previous research was environmental?"

He may actually ask this.

> "The research settings are different, but what I enjoyed wasn't specifically measuring water quality or light pollution — it was asking a question, collecting biological or environmental measurements carefully, interpreting the data and communicating what they meant. Human physiology gives me the chance to apply that process to questions I'm increasingly interested in through anatomy and physiology."

That's probably the cleanest bridge in her résumé.

---

### "What interests you specifically about fatigue?"

> "I like that fatigue sounds simple until you actually try to explain it mechanistically. Two people can show a similar decline in performance for very different physiological reasons, and even within one person the nervous system, muscle and metabolism can all contribute. I find the idea of teasing those mechanisms apart really interesting."

That sounds curious rather than rehearsed.

---

### "Are you comfortable working with human research participants?"

A good answer:

> "Yes. I understand that human-subject research adds responsibilities around standardized procedures, participant comfort, consent, confidentiality and following protocols exactly. I haven't done human testing yet, but I'm comfortable working with people and I'm very willing to be trained properly."

Her hospitality, legal-administration and leadership experience actually support the "comfortable working with people" claim. 

---

### "How much time can you commit?"

She needs to figure this out **before the meeting**.

He will care more about consistency than heroics.

Better:

> "I can reliably commit about X hours each week, and I can plan those in consistent blocks around my classes."

Not:

> "I'm basically available whenever!"

...followed three weeks later by disappearing during midterms.
